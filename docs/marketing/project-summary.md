# 911records.nyc — project summary

*Written 2026-09-14 for reuse in applications and bios. Numbers are as of that date.*

## What it is

911records.nyc is a free, independent explorer over New York City's newly released 9/11 records. In September 2026 the Mayor's Office published about 172,000 pages (24,436 documents) from the agencies that responded to the attacks and ran the clean-up: DEP, FDNY, DDC, DOB, DCAS and the Mayor's Office. The City's own portal is a single search box: no browsing, no dates, no way to pull more than one document at a time. I built 911records.nyc over a weekend so that families, lawyers, journalists and researchers can actually read the collection.

The motivation is personal. My father-in-law died of a 9/11-related cancer. When we tried to get him certified with the Victim Compensation Fund we could not find the paperwork that proved where he had been and what he had been exposed to. It existed in a City filing system; we just could not get to it.

What a visitor can do:

- Search with real filters (agency, box, folder, substance, lab, address, year), with results that show a plain-language title and one-line summary for every document.
- Browse the collection the way the City filed it: collection, box, folder, document, page, with the City's own labels preserved.
- Look up any lab, agency, contractor, substance, address, or official and see every record they appear on, with the role they played.
- Ask a plain-language question ("Which buildings were tested for asbestos?") and get either a written answer in which every sentence cites the exact Bates page it came from, or a table of distinct buildings, labs, contractors or officials with a source link per row. Answers are frozen at a permanent URL with follow-up questions that keep the context of the thread.
- Save pages to a case folder and export an exhibit list for a claim.
- See what the City adds or removes over time: the site re-captures the City's catalog daily and keeps a change log, so a document that disappears is recorded as removed rather than silently vanishing.

It runs on two application hosts behind a Cloudflare tunnel, with a Postgres primary and standby, deploys automatically from the main branch, serves a self-refreshing maintenance page during the few seconds of a deploy, and publishes a user-facing release history (about 45 releases in the first 36 hours). The build made heavy use of parallel AI coding agents, each in its own git worktree with its own ticket, coordinated from one session.

## How it visualises the data

The home page is a 3D map of lower Manhattan, built with MapLibre GL over present-day NYC building footprints. Every building named in the records is highlighted, and the colour says what kind of record exists for it: test candidates (sampling or lab results), inspection candidates, or a mention only. A search bar sits over the map with suggested questions that are checked to actually return results. Clicking a building opens a building file: the records, a month-by-month activity chart with hover values, the tests-over-time table (substances, values found on the page, labs), the documents grouped by what they are (lab reports, chain-of-custody forms, memos, permits, invoices), and present-day building facts (year built, floors, units) from prospect.nyc, clearly labelled as today's data rather than 2001's. The map's default frame runs from the harbour to the Brooklyn Bridge; a toggle switches between 3D massing and a flat plan, and the legend sits beside the controls (below the map on phones).

The topic map is a treemap: page and document embeddings are clustered, each cluster is given a human-readable name by a language model, and the area of each block is the number of pages. Selecting a topic zooms into its sub-topics and then to its documents. It is a map of subjects, deliberately never of people.

Entities are presented as panels (labs, agencies and offices, contractors, substances, addresses and buildings, officials by role) rather than one long list, and each entity page follows the same shape as a building file: a header, activity over time, records grouped by role, and related entities that co-occur on the same pages. Documents everywhere show a machine-written title and summary so a list of Bates numbers becomes a readable inventory.

## How it deals with poor-quality source data

The source material is scanned faxes, hand-annotated lab reports and folder separator sheets from 2001–2003, run through the City's own OCR. Much of it is garbled ("Asbestos Fiber Analysis by Traa smritilsion" for "Transmission"), thousands of pages have no text at all, and the City's folder labels are often bare numbers or block-and-lot strings. The approach is to extract what can be extracted, label everything as machine-derived, and always route the reader to the page image as the authority.

- **Text recovery.** Text is taken from the PDFs with word bounding boxes, so search hits can be highlighted on the page image. Pages with no text layer (about 2,200) are rendered and OCR'd locally; pages that are pure noise (about 3,900) are classified as junk and kept out of search. Coverage figures are shown on the home page.
- **Structure recovery.** The City's one-page property-lookup cover sheets (address, Block/Lot, BIN) are detected with OCR-tolerant rules, hidden from search by default, and used to attribute the whole folder that follows them to the building they name, so a building's lab reports appear on its page even when the report itself never spells the address. Document types (lab report, chain of custody, memo, permit, invoice, photo log) are classified by rules and used to group and label documents.
- **Names and places.** Addresses, labs and contractors are extracted from noisy text and canonicalised in three passes: a normaliser and gazetteer built from a citywide property roll, an OCR-aware fuzzy match, and a language model only as a last resort with guards against swapping one building for another. Every match carries a confidence score, and the "verify" link on a page goes to the source page.
- **Search that survives typos and OCR.** Retrieval is hybrid: BM25 over the text plus k-nearest-neighbour search over 768-dimensional embeddings with cosine similarity, so a question phrased differently from the page ("environmental testing") still finds "asbestos fibre analysis by TEM". Filters match regardless of capitalisation, a filter that matches nothing falls back to the words with a note, and the query router tolerates misspellings before deciding whether a query is a Bates number, a keyword search, or a question.
- **Answers that cannot invent.** The question-answering path retrieves at most twelve pages, builds excerpts around the terms actually asked about, and hands them to a small model with a strict instruction: every sentence must cite a given Bates page. The server then validates every citation and drops any sentence whose citation does not resolve, so what remains is checkable. A "what these records do not establish" block states the gaps, and a gap is described as a gap, never as an absence of records. When a question names a building, retrieval searches inside that building's own documents first, with a note telling the model how the City filed them.
- **Honesty in the interface.** Every derived value is labelled machine-extracted with its confidence; numbers found on a page are labelled as values found on the page, not readings; date spans say "no date extracted" rather than guessing; summaries carry a "verify against the page image" note; and the site serves the records exactly as the City published them, including its redactions, with a report link on every page for anything that should have been redacted and a stated policy of never reconstructing a redacted name.
- **Continuous improvement of the extraction.** Summaries and titles are generated in batches by whichever model is available (a hosted small model, a coding-agent CLI, or a local 35B-parameter model on the same Mac), with results cached by the hash of their inputs so a re-run only re-asks for documents whose text or classification changed. A daily pipeline re-downloads what changed, re-extracts, re-embeds, rebuilds the derived database and swaps it in atomically, so improvements to any extraction stage flow through to the site the next morning.
